## Answer

1. **No form is required, in the EU or the UK.** A plain email is a valid Access Request or Removal Request. An Organization may offer a form or portal but cannot make it compulsory. EU: send it to a channel the Organization itself provides (privacy/DPO address, or a general contact address it publishes); the EDPB says an Organization need not act on mail sent to a random or apparently incorrect address, or to staff not involved in handling requests, if it has provided an appropriate channel. UK: the regulator says a request can be made "to any part of your organisation" and need not go to a specific contact point.
2. **No reasons are needed** for an Access Request, and it covers all the Subject's data unless narrowed. It should ask for confirmation of processing, a copy (Art. 15(3)), and the Art. 15(1)(a)–(h) items — for a Broker, above all the *source* (15(1)(g)) and the *named recipients* (15(1)(c)).
3. **A Removal Request should stack three things**: erasure (Art. 17(1)), objection (Art. 21: the direct-marketing objection in 21(2)–(3) is absolute; a general objection under 21(1) must give grounds relating to the Subject's "particular situation"), and withdrawal of consent (Art. 7(3)). Expect some refusals under the Art. 17(3) exceptions, in particular 17(3)(b) (legal retention duty) and 17(3)(e) (legal claims). The EDPB's 2025 erasure report does not rank the exceptions by frequency; it reports that refusals examined were "overall justified" and that some Organizations apply exceptions automatically without case-by-case assessment.
4. **Deadline: one month from receipt**, extendable by two further months only if the Organization says so, with reasons, inside the first month. Count to the same day-number in the next month (last day of the month if there is none; next working day if it lands on a weekend or public holiday).
5. **Identity checks and clarification requests move the deadline.** EU (EDPB interpretation, not GDPR text): the clock may be suspended while the Organization waits for identity information, if it asked without undue delay; the same applies when it asks the Subject to specify a broad Access Request where it processes a large quantity of data. UK (statute): the month only starts when the requested identity information arrives, and for Access Requests the days from asking for clarification to receiving it (both days included) do not count.
6. **An Organization may ask for more identity information only on reasonable doubt, and only what is proportionate.** The EDPB treats demanding a copy of an ID document as generally inappropriate, and disproportionate where the person is already authenticated.
7. **Someone else can act for a Subject, with authority.** The Organization may verify both identity and authorisation. EU: the GDPR does not regulate representation; national law governs it, and the Organization must be able to demonstrate the authorisation, so it will ask for evidence. UK: "the third party is responsible for providing you with evidence", e.g. signed written authority or a power of attorney. A child's rights belong to the child; a holder of parental responsibility acts for a child who is not mature enough. Art. 8 age thresholds are *consent* ages, not capacity-to-request ages.
8. **No fixed interval for repeating an Access Request exists** in either regime; it depends on how often the data changes, and Organizations that refuse bear the burden of proof. The EDPB's adopted guidelines do give two benchmarks in worked examples: requests every three months to a social-media platform are "in principle" not excessive, and "a one-year interval between information provided free of charge will in any case be too large for the request to be considered excessive" (stated in its credit-agency example). A first request can be refused as excessive only if the Organization demonstrates an abusive intention (CJEU C-526/24, 19 March 2026).
9. **Complaints are free and are lodged by the Subject** with the Supervisory Authority of their residence, workplace or the place of infringement. For cross-border cases the Subject's own authority stays their contact point while a lead authority investigates. Organizations with no EEA establishment get no one-stop-shop.
10. **UK now has a statutory right to complain to the Organization** (DPA 2018 s.164A, in force 19 June 2026: acknowledgement within 30 days of receipt). It is not a legal precondition for complaining to the regulator: the regulator says "You can also complain to us at any time", but recommends giving the Organization a chance to complete its complaint handling first, asks for a copy of that complaint on its complaint form, and suggests complaining to it "within three months of receiving the organisation's response" (later complaints are still considered).
11. **UK specifics in force today**: "reasonable and proportionate search" limit on access (deemed in force from 1 January 2024); statutory time-limit rules incl. stop-the-clock for clarification (in force 5 February 2026 for requests received on or after that date); the office of Information Commissioner was abolished and its functions transferred to the **Information Commission** on 30 September 2026 (SI 2026/1015). The regulator's website still brands itself "ICO".
12. **Two things are moving**: the EU "Digital Omnibus" proposal to loosen Art. 12(5) for "abusive" access requests (EUR-Lex shows only the Commission proposal and advisory opinions; no adoption), and Regulation (EU) 2025/2518, Art. 4 of which fixes what a cross-border complaint must contain from 2 April 2027 and requires that a rights request was made to the Organization before a complaint about it is lodged.

## 1. What a valid request must contain

### 1.1 Form

- **The GDPR sets no form requirement.** "The GDPR does not impose any requirements on data subjects regarding the form of the request for access to the personal data" — EDPB 01/2022 para. 52. The EDPB CEF 2024 report repeats that "the GDPR does not introduce any formal requirements for access requests" and lists, among barriers, that "Some controllers required data subjects to use a specific mechanism (i.e. web-form)".
- **The Organization's own form or portal is optional.** The controller "should provide appropriate and user-friendly communication channels", but "the data subject is not required to use these specific channels and may instead send the request to an official contact point of the controller" — EDPB 01/2022, Executive Summary (not paras. 53–56). Para. 53: a request sent through any communication channel provided by the controller, even one other than the preferred one, "shall be, in general, considered effective". A self-service tool may be encouraged but "the controller must also handle access requests that are not sent through the established channel of communication" — EDPB 01/2022 paras. 137–138.
- **The address matters (EU).** "The controller is not obliged to act on requests that are sent to completely random, or apparently incorrect, addresses" — EDPB 01/2022, Executive Summary. Para. 54 states the condition: no obligation for a request sent to "a random or incorrect e-mail (or postal) address, not directly provided by the controller", or to a channel clearly not meant for rights requests, "if the controller has provided an appropriate communication channel". Para. 55: the same for an employee not involved in handling requests (e.g. drivers, cleaning staff), but not for a regular contact person such as an account manager. Para. 56, Example 8: a request to a published general address must be redirected, and the controller may not extend the period because of it. The clock starts when the request "reaches the controller through one of its official channels" — para. 159.
- **UK: no form, and any part of the Organization.** "There are no formal requirements for a valid request. A person can make a SAR verbally or in writing, including by social media. They can make it to any part of your organisation, and they do not have to direct it to a specific person or contact point." It need not say "subject access request" or cite Article 15; a standard form may be offered but "you should make it clear that using the form or online system is not compulsory" — ICO access, "How do we recognise a subject access request (SAR)?" https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/right-of-access/how-do-we-recognise-a-subject-access-request-sar/
- **Erasure has no form requirement either.** UK: "an individual can make a request for erasure verbally or in writing. It can also be made to any part of your organisation" — ICO erasure. EU: Art. 12(2) GDPR obliges the controller to "facilitate the exercise of data subject rights under Articles 15 to 22" and Art. 17 prescribes no form. The EDPB has no general guideline on Art. 17: its CEF 2025 report (footnote 14) points instead to Guidelines 5/2019 on search engines, whose guidance on Art. 17(1) and (3) "could still be relevant to a certain extent" to other controllers, and to EDPB 01/2022 for Art. 12; for erasure request channels it cites EDPB 01/2022 paras. 52–56.
- **Electronic requests get electronic answers.** Recital 59 GDPR: "The controller should also provide means for requests to be made electronically". Art. 12(3) GDPR: "Where the data subject makes the request by electronic form means, the information shall be provided by electronic means where possible, unless otherwise requested by the data subject." The same sentence remains in UK GDPR Art. 12(3).
- **Free of charge.** Art. 12(5) GDPR; a first copy is free even if no reason is given — CJEU C-307/22 *FT v DW* (26 Oct 2023) https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/en/ALL/?uri=CELEX%3A62022CJ0307

### 1.2 What an Access Request should ask for

- **No reasons required.** Controllers "should not assess “why” the data subject is requesting access, but only “what” the data subject is requesting" — EDPB 01/2022 para. 13. The CJEU held that neither Art. 12(5) nor Art. 15(1), (3) makes the right conditional on stating reasons, and it applies even where the purpose is unrelated to those in Recital 63 — C-307/22.
- **Default scope is everything.** "Unless explicitly requested otherwise by the data subject, a request to exercise the right of access shall be understood in general terms, encompassing all personal data concerning the data subject" — EDPB 01/2022 para. 35. A controller processing a large quantity may ask the person to specify (para. 35(b)), but "the request for specification shall not aim at a limitation of the reply to the access request", and if the person confirms they want everything it must be provided in full — same section, shortly after para. 35.
- **Art. 15(1) GDPR content:** confirmation "as to whether or not" data is processed; access to the data; (a) purposes; (b) categories of data; (c) "the recipients or categories of recipient to whom the personal data have been or will be disclosed, in particular recipients in third countries"; (d) "where possible" the envisaged retention period, or the criteria; (e) existence of the rights to rectification, erasure, restriction and objection; (f) right to lodge a complaint with a Supervisory Authority; (g) "where the personal data are not collected from the data subject, any available information as to their **source**"; (h) existence of automated decision-making including profiling, with "meaningful information about the logic involved" and its significance and envisaged consequences. Art. 15(2): the right to be informed of the Art. 46 safeguards where data is transferred to a third country. UK GDPR Art. 15(1) additionally lists, since 19 June 2026, "(ea) the right to make a complaint to the controller under section 164A of the 2018 Act" (DUAA Sch. 10 para. 5).
- **Recipients by name.** Art. 15(1)(c) obliges the controller "to provide the actual identity of those recipients" unless identification is impossible or the request is manifestly unfounded or excessive — CJEU C-154/21 *RW v Österreichische Post* (12 Jan 2023) https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=celex%3A62021CJ0154
- **Copy means a real copy.** Art. 15(3) requires "a faithful and intelligible reproduction of all the personal data undergoing processing"; a general description or list of categories is not enough — CJEU C-487/21 *F.F. v Österreichische Datenschutzbehörde* (4 May 2023) https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/en/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A62021CJ0487
- **A negative answer is still an answer.** Art. 15(1) starts with the right to "confirmation as to whether or not personal data concerning him or her are being processed", so "we hold nothing" is a response the Organization owes (the conclusion is a reading of that text).
- **UK limit:** the right extends only to what the controller can provide after "a reasonable and proportionate search" — UK GDPR Art. 15(1A), inserted by DUAA s.78 https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2025/18/section/78

### 1.3 Art. 17(1) grounds for a Removal Request

Art. 17(1) GDPR grounds: (a) data no longer necessary for the purposes collected; (b) consent withdrawn and no other legal ground; (c) objection under Art. 21(1) with no overriding legitimate grounds, or objection under Art. 21(2); (d) data unlawfully processed; (e) "the personal data have to be erased for compliance with a legal obligation in Union or Member State law to which the controller is subject"; (f) "the personal data have been collected in relation to the offer of information society services referred to in Article 8(1)" (Art. 8(1) concerns a child's consent; the text of (f) does not itself say "from a child"). All six checked against the GDPR text; see also CJEU C-26/22 and C-64/22 *SCHUFA* https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A62022CJ0026.

Which grounds fit which Organization is a reading of the text, not something a source states:

| Situation | Grounds that fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Broker** (never had a relationship with the Subject; typically relies on legitimate interests) | 17(1)(c) via Art. 21(2) if data is used for direct marketing — absolute; 17(1)(c) via Art. 21(1) otherwise — the Broker must show overriding legitimate grounds; 17(1)(d) if there was never a valid legal basis; 17(1)(b) if the Broker claims consent | Objection is the natural route against legitimate-interest processing |
| **Service the Subject no longer uses** | 17(1)(a) once the account is closed and the purpose is spent; 17(1)(b) for consent-based processing (newsletters, tracking); 17(1)(c) via Art. 21(2) for marketing | The request should also close the account, otherwise contract necessity remains a basis |

### 1.4 Art. 17(3) exceptions Organizations invoke

- Art. 17(3) GDPR: erasure does not apply "to the extent that processing is necessary" (a) for freedom of expression and information; (b) to comply with a legal obligation under Union or Member State law, or to perform a public-interest task or exercise official authority; (c) for public health (Art. 9(2)(h), (i), 9(3)); (d) for archiving, research or statistics, where erasure would render impossible or seriously impair those objectives; (e) "for the establishment, exercise or defence of legal claims" — all checked against the GDPR text.
- **No frequency ranking in the EDPB report.** The EDPB's 2025 coordinated action (32 authorities, 764 controllers) does not say which exception is used most; the report and its press release contain no ranking. What it does say (EDPB CEF 2025, section 3 and Issue 4): authorities that carried out enforcement found "the rejection of requests by responding controllers was overall justified"; some controllers treat exceptions "as automatically applicable without conducting a case-by-case assessment"; it names problems with 17(3)(a) (media outlets citing freedom of expression without a documented balancing), 17(3)(b) (failing to assess the specific retention duty in each case) and refusals on "legitimate interest" grounds without a documented balancing test. It recommends that refusals be documented with legal reasoning.
- The same report lists seven recurring issues, including weak internal erasure procedures, insufficient information to data subjects, anonymising instead of deleting, retention periods and backups.
- Organizations may also refuse under Art. 12(5) (manifestly unfounded or excessive) — see section 5.

### 1.5 How objection (Art. 21) and consent withdrawal (Art. 7(3)) relate

- **Direct marketing objection is absolute.** "Where personal data are processed for direct marketing purposes, the data subject shall have the right to object at any time ... which includes profiling to the extent that it is related to such direct marketing" (Art. 21(2)); then "the personal data shall no longer be processed for such purposes" (Art. 21(3)) — GDPR https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng. ICO object: "This is an absolute right and there are no exemptions or grounds for you to refuse."
- **General objection is conditional.** Art. 21(1): the data subject may object "on grounds relating to his or her particular situation, at any time" to processing based on Art. 6(1)(e) or (f), including profiling; "The controller shall no longer process the personal data unless the controller demonstrates compelling legitimate grounds for the processing which override the interests, rights and freedoms of the data subject or for the establishment, exercise or defence of legal claims." So a 21(1) objection should say something about the Subject's own situation; a 21(2) marketing objection needs no grounds.
- **Objection feeds erasure.** A successful objection is itself an erasure ground: Art. 17(1)(c).
- **Objection alone is not erasure.** After a marketing objection an Organization normally *suppresses* rather than deletes: it keeps the minimum needed to remember not to market to the person. "You should not delete a person's information from a suppression list" — ICO object. This matters for Brokers that re-acquire data: a suppression record is what keeps a Subject removed.
- **Consent withdrawal.** Consent can be withdrawn at any time and "it shall be as easy to withdraw as to give consent" (Art. 7(3) GDPR). Withdrawal triggers erasure ground 17(1)(b) where there is "no other legal ground". "The withdrawal of consent shall not affect the lawfulness of processing based on consent before its withdrawal" (Art. 7(3)).
- **Passing it on.** Art. 19 GDPR: the controller "shall communicate any rectification or erasure ... carried out in accordance with ... Article 17(1) ... to each recipient to whom the personal data have been disclosed, unless this proves impossible or involves disproportionate effort. The controller shall inform the data subject about those recipients if the data subject requests it." ICO erasure says the same. Art. 17(2) is narrower: where the controller *made the data public*, it must take reasonable steps to inform other controllers processing it of the erasure request. In C-129/21 *Proximus* (27 Oct 2022), on telephone directories, the CJEU held that a Supervisory Authority *may require* a controller to take measures to inform the third-party controllers it received data from or supplied it to of a consent withdrawal, and that 17(2) does not preclude an order to inform search engines — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A62021CJ0129. (The earlier wording here, that the controller "must communicate" a withdrawal to others relying on the same consent, overstated the ruling.)

## 2. Deadlines

- **One month.** The controller must inform the person of the action taken on the request "without undue delay and in any event within one month of receipt of the request" — Art. 12(3) GDPR as quoted in EDPB 01/2022. Recital 59 says the same. This applies to requests under Arts. 15–22, so to both Access and Removal Requests.
- **Extension.** "That period may be extended by two further months where necessary, taking into account the complexity and number of the requests. The controller shall inform the data subject of any such extension within one month of receipt of the request, together with the reasons for the delay" — Art. 12(3) GDPR. UK equivalent, UK GDPR Art. 12A(3)–(4): notice "before the end of the period of one month beginning with the relevant time" and it must "state the reasons for the delay".
- **Refusal also has a deadline.** "If the controller does not take action on the request of the data subject, the controller shall inform the data subject without delay and at the latest within one month of receipt of the request of the reasons for not taking action and on the possibility of lodging a complaint with a supervisory authority and seeking a judicial remedy" — Art. 12(4) GDPR. UK GDPR Art. 12(4) now requires mention of complaining to the controller under s.164A, to the regulator under s.165, and a judicial remedy, within the applicable time period.
- **Clock start (EU).** "The time limit starts when the controller has received an Art. 15 request, meaning when the request reaches the controller through one of its official channels. It is not necessary that the controller is in fact aware of the request" — EDPB 01/2022 para. 159.
- **Counting the month (EU).** "The time period to respond to an access request needs to be calculated in accordance with Regulation No 1182/71" — EDPB 01/2022 para. 160, with Example 32 (received 5 March: "until and including 5 April") and Example 33 (received 31 August: 30 September). Para. 161: "If the last day of this time period falls on a weekend or a public holiday, the controller has until the next working day to respond." Footnote 90 adds that in some member states national law determines when a message counts as received, taking account of weekends and holidays. Para. 162: the two-month extension "should not be overused". Under Reg. 1182/71 Art. 3(2)(c) a period in months ends on the day with the same date as the starting day, or on the last day of the month if that date does not exist; Art. 3(4) rolls a period ending on a Saturday, Sunday or public holiday to the next working day; Art. 3(3) counts weekends and holidays inside the period.
- **Identity verification and clarification (EU).** Where the controller needs to check identity, "there may be a suspension in time until the controller has obtained the information needed from the data subject, provided the controller has asked for additional information without undue delay. The same applies for when a controller has asked a data subject to specify the processing operations to which the request relates, when the conditions set out in Recital 63 are met" — EDPB 01/2022 para. 159 (Recital 63: a controller processing "a large quantity of information" may ask the data subject to specify). Example 31: a second round of identity questions extends the suspension. The GDPR text itself says nothing about pausing; this is EDPB interpretation.
- **Speed.** The month is a maximum: "the information should be given as soon as possible. This means that, if it is possible to provide the requested information in a shorter amount of time than one month, the controller should do so" — EDPB 01/2022 para. 158.

**UK**

- **Statutory rule.** "The applicable time period" is "the period of one month beginning with the relevant time". The relevant time is the latest of: when the controller receives the request; when it receives "the information (if any) requested in connection with a request under Article 12(6)" (identity); and when any Art. 12(5) fee is paid — UK GDPR Art. 12A, inserted by DUAA s.76 https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2025/18/section/76. So in the UK the month **starts** on receipt of requested identity information; it is a fresh start, not a pause. DUAA s.76 also amended UK GDPR Art. 12(6): on reasonable doubt the controller may request identity information "and (b) delay dealing with the request until the identity is confirmed". In force 5 February 2026 (SI 2026/82 reg. 2(h)); by reg. 4 the old time limits still apply to requests received before that date.
- **Stop the clock for clarification.** Where the controller "reasonably requires further information in order to identify the information or processing activities to which a request under Article 15 relates", "the period beginning with the day on which the controller makes the request and ending with the day on which the controller receives the information does not count" towards the time period or the extension-notice deadline — UK GDPR Art. 12A(5), inserted by DUAA s.76. Art. 12A(6) gives as an example a controller that processes a large amount of information about the person. The controller must be able to show clarification is reasonably required — ICO DUAA summary https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/what-we-do/legislation-we-cover/data-use-and-access-act-2025/the-data-use-and-access-act-2025-duaa-summary-of-the-changes/data-protection/. This pause is for access requests only, and only for clarification about the information requested, not e.g. format — ICO access, "What should we consider when responding to a request?"
- **Counting.** "start from the actual date you receive the request, fee or other requested information and count forward to the end of the same date in the following month. Even if you receive the request on a non-working day, you must start from this date." "If the same date doesn't exist in the following month (because it's shorter), use the last day of that month instead." "If the date for responding falls on a weekend or is a public holiday, the deadline moves to the end of the next working day." — ICO access, "What should we consider when responding to a request?" https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/right-of-access/what-should-we-consider-when-responding-to-a-request/
- **When a stopped clock resumes.** The statute excludes both the day of asking and the day of receiving clarification, so the clock resumes the day after receipt. The current ICO page agrees: "the time limit pauses on the day you request clarification and resumes on the day after you receive it", with the worked example received 14 May, clarification asked 15 May and received 18 May, "you can extend the original one-month deadline by four days, and your response is due by or on 18 June". The 17 June version seen in other ICO material earlier does not match the statute; it was not located in this pass.

## 3. Identity verification

- **Rule.** "Where the controller has reasonable doubts concerning the identity of the natural person making the request referred to in Articles 15 to 21, the controller may request the provision of additional information necessary to confirm the identity of the data subject" — Art. 12(6) GDPR, "without prejudice to Article 11".
- **Recital 64.** "The controller should use all reasonable measures to verify the identity of a data subject who requests access, in particular in the context of online services and online identifiers. A controller should not retain personal data for the sole purpose of being able to react to potential requests."
- **Art. 11.** A controller that cannot identify the person need not acquire extra data just to comply; then Arts. 15–20 "shall not apply except where the data subject ... provides additional information enabling his or her identification" (Art. 11(2)). Art. 12(2): in the cases in Art. 11(2) "the controller shall not refuse to act on the request ... unless the controller demonstrates that it is not in a position to identify the data subject".
- **EDPB 01/2022** on how much may be asked:
  - The controller is not obliged to acquire extra information merely to identify someone, but should not refuse additional information the person provides — para. 61.
  - Additional information may be requested on reasonable doubt — paras. 63 and 70 (para. 66 is about using existing authentication procedures); the controller must assess each time what would confirm identity and ask only where proportionate — para. 67; it must say what kind of information it needs, and that "should not be more than the information initially needed for the authentication of the data subject" — para. 68.
  - The authentication method "should be relevant, appropriate, proportionate and respect the data minimisation principle"; burdensome measures must be justified — para. 71 (para. 70 sets out the proportionality assessment).
  - "It is disproportionate to require a copy of an identity document in the event where the data subject making a request is already authenticated by the controller" — para. 73.
  - Using a copy of an identity document "creates a risk for the security of personal data ... and as such it should be considered inappropriate, unless it is necessary, suitable, and in line with national law" — para. 74. ID copies should "generally not be considered an appropriate way of authentication" — para. 75.
  - Information on an ID that is not needed (e.g. access and serial number, nationality, photo, machine-readable zone) "may be redacted or hidden by the data subject before submitting it", except where national law requires a full copy; the controller should tell the person they may redact — paras. 76–77. ID-based authentication may be justified for special-category or risky processing — para. 78. Where ID is requested lawfully, the controller should refrain from copying or delete the copy after authentication, and as good practice note e.g. "ID card was checked" — para. 79.
- **Enforcement practice.** "An indiscriminate request for an identification document for all access requests may constitute excessive processing of personal data"; "The controller should be able to demonstrate the reasonable doubt if requested"; controllers should be made aware "of their obligation to demonstrate their need for further identification documents" — EDPB CEF 2024.
- **UK.** Be reasonable and proportionate; "Only request formal identification documents if necessary. You can use verification measures that you already have in place (eg an existing username and password)"; if identity is obvious more is unlikely to be needed; asking for other account details is reasonable where details do not match; "you do not need to keep copies of ID documents" — ICO access, "What should we consider when responding to a request?" (section "Can we ask for ID?").

## 4. Acting for someone else

- **Allowed.** "Although the right of access is generally exercised by the data subjects as it pertains to them, it is possible for a third party to make a request on behalf of the data subject. This may apply to, among others, acting through a proxy or legal guardians on behalf of minors, as well as acting through other entities via online portals" — EDPB 01/2022 para. 80. Identity and authorisation may be verified "where it is suitable and proportionate". Para. 81: "national laws governing legal representation (e.g. powers of attorney), which may impose specific requirements for demonstrating authorisation ... should be taken into account, since the GDPR does not regulate this issue"; controllers "shall be able to demonstrate the existence of the relevant authorisation", so it is "recommended to collect appropriate documentation".
- **Portals.** A controller first ensures the third party is acting legitimately for the person (para. 89). It must handle the request "in a timely manner", but "There is, however, no obligation for the controller to provide the data under Art. 15 GDPR directly to the portal, if the controller, for example, establishes that the security measures are insufficient or it would be deemed appropriate to use another way for the disclosure of data to the data subject" — EDPB 01/2022 paras. 88–90.
- **UK authority.** The Organization must be satisfied the third party is entitled to act; "The third party is responsible for providing you with evidence of this — for example, by providing a written authority, signed by the person the information is about", or a power of attorney; electronically signed letters of authority may be accepted under conditions (recent, clearly made by the person). Without evidence the Organization "cannot comply with the SAR until you receive the appropriate authority". Once satisfied, it should "in most cases" respond directly to the third party; it may first contact the person if the information is sensitive — ICO access, "How do we recognise a SAR?". A request by a representative is treated "as if the person themselves had made the request", so "you cannot take into account other requests made by the representative on behalf of other people"; "A SAR made as part of a bulk request has the same legal status as one person making a SAR" — ICO access, "What should we consider when responding to a request?" (section on bulk requests).
- **UK portals.** "If you can't view the request without paying a fee or signing up for a service, you haven't received the SAR and don't have to respond." Agreement to a portal's terms and conditions "is unlikely to be evidence of appropriate authority". If the Organization will not use the portal (fees, sign-up, security), it should provide the information directly to the person if they agree; a person may use a portal but ask for delivery another way — ICO access, "How do we recognise a SAR?".
- **UK, AI-assisted requests** (ICO brief guidance, published 11 November 2026): an AI-assisted request must be treated like any other and "isn't invalid just because it contains inaccurate or false information"; if a controller thinks a letter of authority was generated with AI it "should contact the person the request is about directly"; controllers "could also ask people to review any AI-assisted SAR before sending it"; using AI does not by itself make a request manifestly unfounded or excessive — https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2026/11/subject-access-requests-generated-by-artificial-intelligence/. The page does not mention further guidance planned for winter 2026; that statement has been removed.
- **Adults lacking capacity (UK).** Neither the UK GDPR nor the mental-capacity statutes contain a specific provision letting a third party make a request for them; an attorney with authority over property and affairs can reasonably be assumed to have authority — ICO access.

**Children**

- "Children are data subjects in their own right and, as such, the right of access belongs to the child. Depending on the maturity and capacity of the child, the child may need a third party to act on its behalf e.g. the holder of parental responsibility" — EDPB 01/2022 para. 84. The child's best interests lead (paras. 85–86). Parents' data-protection standing is separate from other national-law rights to information about a child (para. 87).
- **UK.** The Organization decides whether the child is competent, i.e. "mature enough to understand their rights"; if so it should respond to the child. If not, "it's usually appropriate to let the holder of parental responsibility exercise the child's rights on their behalf, unless you have evidence that this is not in the child's best interests". A competent child may also authorise someone else (e.g. a parent or solicitor) to request for them. "In Scotland, a person aged 12 or over is usually considered to be mature enough to exercise these rights unless there is evidence to suggest otherwise. This presumption doesn't apply in England, Wales or Northern Ireland" — ICO access, "How do we recognise a SAR?" (children section amended 7 April 2026). The current page does not mention birth or adoption certificates as evidence of parental responsibility; that statement has been removed. The Scottish presumption is in DPA 2018 s.208(3): "A person aged 12 or over is to be presumed to be of sufficient age and maturity to have such understanding, unless the contrary is shown"; s.208 covers both exercising a right and giving consent.
- **Age thresholds are about consent, not requests.** Art. 8(1) GDPR sets 16 as the age at which a child can consent to an information society service, and "Member States may provide by law for a lower age for those purposes provided that such lower age is not below 13 years". It does not set an age for exercising Art. 15 or 17; that turns on maturity and national law. Reported thresholds — 16: DE, HR, HU, IE, LU, NL, PL, RO, SK; 15: CZ, EL, FR; 14: AT, BG, CY, ES, IT, LT (Commission-commissioned study, June 2020, https://commission.europa.eu/document/download/837d04b9-7e71-4534-8eaa-599902ea28dc_en?filename=GDPR+Study+Report-Tipik+-June+2020.pdf); 13: BE, DK, EE, FI, LV, MT, PT, SE (whole member-state list unverified against source). A late-2025 Commission report counts 7/6/5/9 states at 13/14/15/16, so at least one state has changed since 2020 (unverified against source). UK: 13, now written into UK GDPR Art. 8(1) itself; DPA 2018 s.9, which set it originally, was omitted on 31 December 2020. Treat any hard-coded table as stale.

## 5. Repeat requests

- **Rule.** Requests are free, but where they are "manifestly unfounded or excessive, in particular because of their repetitive character", the controller may charge a reasonable fee or refuse; "The controller shall bear the burden of demonstrating the manifestly unfounded or excessive character of the request" — Art. 12(5) GDPR. Recital 63 speaks of exercising the right "easily and at reasonable intervals".
- **No fixed interval (EU).** "It is not possible to generally determine any specific interval in which the submission of a further access request could be deemed excessive under Art. 12(5) second sentence GDPR. An overall consideration of the circumstances of the individual case is rather required" — EDPB 01/2022, within para. 185 (Example 41, credit agencies). Para. 183: whether "the threshold of reasonable intervals (see Recital 63) has been exceeded" depends on the circumstances of each case.
- **What drives it.** "The more often changes occur in the database of the controller, the more often data subjects may be permitted to request access to their personal data without it being excessive" — para. 184; a social network changes faster than a land register. Factors (para. 185): how often the data is altered, the nature of the data, the purposes of processing, whether later requests concern the same information. Example 39: asking a carpenter every two months "may as a consequence be regarded as excessive". Para. 186: where information can be provided easily by electronic means, later requests are unlikely to be excessive.
- **Two benchmarks the adopted v2.1 does keep.** Example 40 (social media platform, within para. 185): "Access requests every three months may - under these circumstances - in principle not be regarded as excessive due to repetitiveness." Example 41 (credit agencies, within para. 185): "it can be assumed that a one-year interval between information provided free of charge will in any case be too large for the request to be considered excessive"; within a very short interval, "the decisive factor should be whether the data subject has reason to assume that the information or the processing has changed" (e.g. after taking a loan). The original note said these could not be found in v2.1; they are there, though only inside worked examples.
- **A free copy is owed even if nothing changed.** Example 2 (a new request one year after the reply): "Even if no change in the data processing by the company occurred– which is not necessarily apparent to the data subject – the data subject has the right to get a free copy of the data" — EDPB 01/2022 para. 28. A repeat that covers the same processing "in terms of time and scope" is a request for an additional copy, for which a fee may be charged (para. 28; Art. 15(3)). A repeat because the first answer was incomplete or unreasoned is "merely a reminder" (para. 29).
- **Fee before refusal.** For excessive requests "it will often be more appropriate to charge a fee as a compensation for the administrative costs the repetitive requests are causing" — EDPB 01/2022 para. 192 (controllers are not generally obliged to charge a fee before refusing).
- **First requests and abuse.** A *first* request may be regarded as "excessive" where the controller "demonstrates, in the light of all the relevant circumstances of the case" that it was made "not for the purpose of being aware of the processing ... but with an abusive intention, such as that of artificially creating the conditions laid down for obtaining an advantage from that regulation"; that the person has made "a large number of requests for access ... followed by claims for compensation, to various controllers" may be taken into account. The concept is "interpreted restrictively" and relied on "only exceptionally" — CJEU C-526/24 *Brillen Rottler* (19 March 2026) https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A62024CJ0526
- **Numbers alone never suffice.** Requests "cannot be classified as 'excessive' solely on account of their number during a specific period"; abusive intention must be shown — CJEU C-416/23 (9 Jan 2025), decided on complaints to a Supervisory Authority under Art. 57(4) https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=celex:62023CJ0416
- **UK.** "there is a high threshold for relying on the manifestly unfounded or excessive provisions"; "Do not assume that a request is manifestly unfounded or excessive simply because the person has previously submitted such a request." Relevant: whether "the request largely repeats previous requests, a reasonable interval hasn't elapsed, and the person's circumstances haven't changed"; for the interval, "The nature of the information" and "How often you alter the information". Repeating can be valid "if the organisation did not handle previous requests properly, or if the response to a previous request revealed new information" — ICO access, "When can we consider a SAR to be manifestly unfounded or excessive?" https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/right-of-access/when-can-we-consider-a-sar-to-be-manifestly-unfounded-or-excessive/. That using AI to draft does not by itself make a request manifestly unfounded or excessive is stated in the ICO's AI-assisted SAR guidance ("Can an AI-assisted SAR be manifestly unfounded or excessive?"), not on this page. UK GDPR Art. 12(5)'s "manifestly unfounded or excessive, in particular because of their repetitive character" wording and the controller's burden of proof are unchanged by DUAA.
- **Pending.** The Commission's Digital Omnibus proposal (COM(2025) 837, procedure 2025/0360(COD), adopted by the Commission 19 November 2025) would let controllers refuse or charge for access requests seen as abusive, including those pursuing aims unrelated to data protection. EDPB–EDPS Joint Opinion 2/2026 (adopted 10 February 2026, paras. 53–57) agrees with the aim but calls the link to "purposes other than the protection of personal data" problematic and asks that the current burden-of-proof threshold be kept — https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-02/edpb_edps_jointopinion_202602_digitalomnibus_en.pdf. The EUR-Lex procedure page (https://eur-lex.europa.eu/procedure/EN/2025_360, read 2026-11-26) lists only the Commission proposal and the ECB, EESC and Committee of the Regions opinions; no adoption by Parliament and Council and no publication in the Official Journal. The claim about Council documents to 31 July 2026 could not be checked (Council site not on the allowed hosts; unverified against source).

## 6. Complaints

**EU**

- **Right and venue.** "Every data subject shall have the right to lodge a complaint with a supervisory authority, in particular in the Member State of his or her habitual residence, place of work or place of the alleged infringement" — Art. 77(1) GDPR https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/art_77/oj. The authority must inform the complainant of progress and outcome (Art. 77(2)).
- **Who lodges.** The right belongs to the data subject. Art. 80(1) lets the data subject mandate a properly constituted not-for-profit body active in data protection to lodge the complaint for them. The GDPR says nothing about other representatives before a Supervisory Authority; the EDPB complaint form provides for "legal representatives" with "document(s) demonstrating your capacity to file this complaint (e.g. power of attorney, mandate ...)", and each authority's national procedure decides the rest (national rules not checked; unverified against source). The Operator is not an Art. 80 body.
- **Free.** Handling is free for the data subject under Art. 57(3), which the CJEU calls the "free-of-charge principle"; Art. 57(4) lets an authority charge or refuse for manifestly unfounded or excessive requests — C-416/23.
- **If the authority stalls.** A judicial remedy is available where the authority does not handle the complaint or does not inform the person within three months — Art. 78(2) GDPR.
- **One-stop-shop.** For cross-border processing "the supervisory authority of the main establishment or of the single establishment of the controller or processor shall be competent to act as lead supervisory authority" (Art. 56(1) GDPR), and it is "the sole interlocutor of the controller or processor" (Art. 56(6)). Art. 56(2) lets the local authority handle a complaint that relates only to an establishment in its state or substantially affects only its residents. Under Art. 60(7)–(8) "The supervisory authority with which a complaint has been lodged shall inform the complainant on the decision", and itself adopts any decision dismissing or rejecting the complaint. The EDPB one-stop-shop leaflet illustrates this: complainants in Italy complain to the Italian authority about a company established in Sweden, and the Italian authority "informs [them] in their own language" — https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2021-06/2020_06_22_one-stop-shop_leaflet_en.pdf. The Subject therefore complains at home in their own language.
- **No one-stop-shop for non-EEA Organizations.** "Controllers without any establishment in the EEA must deal with local supervisory authorities in every Member State they are active in, through their local representative"; an Art. 27 representative does not create a lead authority — EDPB 8/2022. Relevant to US-based Brokers.
- **What a complaint contains today.** National procedure decides. The EDPB template form ("the use of this form is not mandatory"; each authority indicates which fields are mandatory) asks for: whether the filer is the affected person or a representative; name, address, email, phone; the Organization's name, contact details and registration number; facts "in chronological order", the alleged infringement and "the remedy/action that you seek"; whether the Organization or its data protection officer was contacted before the complaint, with "all exchanges, including any replies" attached, or why not; and attachments such as "copy of the request for the exercised right(s) and documentation proving the date the entity became aware of the request; when the request was made via email, a copy of the message sent to the entity showing the date and email addresses of sender and recipient; exhaustion of a deadline", and for legal representatives proof of capacity (e.g. power of attorney) — EDPB complaint form. Its note "for SAs which have such contact as a requirement" confirms that some authorities require prior contact with the Organization.
- **From 2 April 2027 (cross-border complaints).** Reg. 2025/2518 **Art. 4** ("Complaints concerning cross-border processing"): a complaint is admissible if it includes (a) the complainant's name and contact details, (b)–(c) for an Art. 80 body, proof of constitution and, under Art. 80(1), its details and the data subject's mandate, (d) "information which facilitates the identification of the controller or processor", and (e) "a description of the alleged infringement". "No information additional to that referred to in the first subparagraph shall be required", but national "Administrative modalities and requirements" (recital: language, time limits, means of identification, electronic form, template, signature) "shall continue to apply". Art. 4(2): two weeks to declare a deficient complaint inadmissible, with reasons. Art. 4(3): "The complainant shall not be required to have contacted the party under investigation before lodging a complaint", **but** "where a complaint relates to the exercise of a right of the data subject that relies on the data subject concerned making a request to the controller, that request shall be made to the controller before that complaint is lodged". Art. 4(5): an admissible complaint is passed to the presumed lead authority within six weeks, absent early resolution. The Regulation applies from 2 April 2027; Chapters III–IV apply to complaints lodged after that date — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2025/2518/oj/eng
- **Courts in parallel.** Art. 77(1) gives the right to complain "Without prejudice to any other administrative or judicial remedy", and Art. 79(1) gives the right to a judicial remedy against the controller "Without prejudice to any available administrative or non-judicial remedy, including the right to lodge a complaint with a supervisory authority pursuant to Article 77". Proceedings may be brought where the controller is established or where the data subject habitually resides (Art. 79(2)). That the two may run side by side is a reading of that text; CJEU case law on it was not checked.

**UK**

- **Step 1, the Organization.** "A data subject may make a complaint to the controller" about an infringement; the controller must facilitate complaints "such as providing a complaint form which can be completed electronically and by other means", "acknowledge receipt of the complaint within the period of 30 days beginning when the complaint is received", and "without undue delay" take appropriate steps to respond and inform the complainant of the outcome — DPA 2018 s.164A, inserted by DUAA s.103 https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2018/12/section/164A. In force 19 June 2026; the acknowledgement and response duties (s.164A(3)–(4)) apply to complaints received on or after that date — SI 2026/82 regs. 3(a) and 7. The regulator's public guidance counts the 30 days "starting the day after it receives it", rolling a weekend or public holiday to the next working day, and stresses that the 30 days is for acknowledgement, not resolution; no time limit is set for the outcome — https://ico.org.uk/for-the-public/how-to-make-a-data-protection-complaint/
- **Step 2, the regulator.** DPA 2018 s.165(2): "A data subject may make a complaint to the Commission"; UK GDPR Art. 77 was omitted by DUAA s.103(6). The regulator: "You can also complain to us at any time, but we recommend giving the organisation a chance to complete its complaint handling process first" (how-to-make-a-data-protection-complaint page). Its complaint form asks for "a copy of the complaint you made to the organisation", a copy of the original request, a timeline, harm suffered and "proof of authority if you're complaining on behalf of someone else"; "We suggest that you raise your complaint within three months of receiving the organisation's response. However, we will still consider complaints submitted after this period" — https://ico.org.uk/make-a-complaint/data-protection-complaints/. The earlier wording here ("follow up if there is no reply after 30 days", "within three months of your last contact", "last meaningful contact") was not found on the current pages; https://ico.org.uk/make-a-complaint/what-to-expect/ now shows the regulator's complaint-handling framework instead, which "recommend[s] that people give the organisation a chance to put things right". The regulator says it triages complaints and assigns those needing detailed work to case officers "around 10 months after submission".
- **Is step 1 legally mandatory?** No. DPA 2018 ss.164A and 165 contain no precondition, and the regulator says a person can complain to it "at any time". It is the regulator's recommendation, and in practice its form expects the earlier complaint.
- **Free.** UK GDPR Art. 57(3): performance of the regulator's tasks "is to be free of charge for the data subject", subject to DPA 2018 s.135, under which the regulator may charge a reasonable fee or refuse to act on a request that is manifestly unfounded or excessive (e.g. one that "merely repeats the substance of previous requests"), with the burden on the regulator.
- **No one-stop-shop.** UK GDPR Art. 56 and Chapter VII (incl. Art. 60) were omitted on 31 December 2020, so the UK is outside the EU cooperation mechanism. That a UK Subject complains to the UK regulator and an EU Subject to an EU authority is a reading of that.

## Implications for Unlist

**Sending requests**

- Send plain email; never depend on an Organization's form or portal. Do keep, per Organization, a contact address the Organization itself publishes (privacy/DPO address preferred) in the Organization List: in the EU, mail to a random or apparently incorrect address need not be acted on and does not start the clock. The UK is more generous (any part of the Organization), but the same rule keeps behaviour uniform.
- Do not include reasons in an Access Request. Ask for everything: confirmation, a copy, and each Art. 15(1) item, calling out source and named recipients. The answers identify further Organizations to add.
- An Access Request must treat "we hold nothing" as a valid, terminal answer (no Listing).
- A Removal Request should combine erasure (Art. 17(1), citing the fitting grounds per Organization type), objection (Art. 21(2) for marketing, which needs no reasons; Art. 21(1) otherwise, which must state grounds relating to the Subject's particular situation, so the template needs a slot the Subject fills or confirms), consent withdrawal (Art. 7(3)), account closure where there is one, a request to notify recipients (Art. 19), and a request to be told who those recipients are (Art. 19, last sentence). It should accept — or ask for — a minimal suppression record at Brokers, since that is what stops re-listing.
- Model "refused under Art. 17(3)" (most likely (b) or (e)) as a possible outcome with partial erasure, to be checked rather than accepted: the EDPB found refusals overall justified but also found exceptions applied automatically without case-by-case assessment. Record which exception and which legal duty the Organization names. Model "refused under Art. 12(5)" separately; the Organization must give reasons (Art. 12(4)).
- Keep request text identical in legal substance for EU and UK but switch citations (GDPR vs UK GDPR) and the regulator's name.

**Deadlines**

- Record a send timestamp and keep the sent message with headers: Supervisory Authorities ask for proof of when the Organization received the request.
- Compute due date as the same day-number in the following month, falling back to the last day of the month, then rolling past weekends and public holidays of the Organization's country. Store the rule used.
- Model three events that move the due date: an extension notice (plus two months, valid only if received inside the first month and reasoned); an identity-information request (EU: pause from ask to reply, per EDPB; UK: fresh month from reply, per statute); a clarification request about the scope of an Access Request (EU: pause, per EDPB para. 159, where the Organization processes a large quantity of data; UK: pause by statute, access only, excluding both the day asked and the day answered, so add the number of days from ask to answer inclusive).
- UK: the statutory rules apply only to requests received on or after 5 February 2026; nothing sent by Unlist predates that, so no legacy rule is needed.
- Reply to identity or clarification requests fast: until the Subject answers, the clock is not running against the Organization.
- Add a grace margin before treating an Organization as overdue, because of the counting ambiguities above.

**Identity**

- Send from the address the Organization knows the Subject by where possible: it is the cheapest authentication and makes an ID demand harder to justify.
- Never attach ID documents pre-emptively. When an Organization demands a copy of ID, surface it to the Subject as a decision, offer a reply citing Art. 12(6) and EDPB 01/2022 paras. 73–75 (and the EDPB CEF 2024 finding that indiscriminate ID demands may be excessive), and if ID is sent, support redaction of the fields EDPB 01/2022 para. 76 lists as not needed.
- Expect Organizations to ask for information that distinguishes the Subject (previous addresses, customer number); store these per Subject so replies are quick.

**Acting for Subjects**

- Each adult Subject must authorise the Operator. Record that authorisation (who, when, scope) and be able to produce a signed authority: UK guidance puts the burden of providing evidence on the representative, and in the EU the Organization must be able to demonstrate the authorisation, so it will ask; national law may add form requirements. Make the authority recent and specific, since the ICO accepts electronic signatures only if recent and not reused.
- Because mail goes from the Subject's own address, the request reads as made by the Subject. It must therefore really be theirs: have the Subject review and approve what is sent in their name. UK guidance on AI-assisted requests explicitly anticipates Organizations contacting the person directly to check.
- For child Subjects, record who holds parental responsibility and the child's age; do not auto-apply a consent-age table to decide who may request. Flag older children (UK: competent; Scotland: 12 and over) as needing the child's own involvement.
- Do not present Unlist as a portal the Organization must log into to answer; answers should come back to the Subject's mailbox.

**Repeat requests**

- Do not describe any re-request interval as "legally safe"; no rule fixes one. Make the interval configurable per Organization. Defaults can lean on the EDPB's own examples: a year or more between free answers is, in its words, "in any case" too long to be excessive (credit-agency example), and every three months is "in principle" not excessive for a platform whose data changes constantly (social-media example). So: 12 months as the conservative default for Organizations whose data rarely changes; down to 3 months for Brokers and other fast-changing data sets. Allow an earlier repeat when the previous answer was incomplete (that is a reminder, not a new request) or when the Subject has reason to think the data changed.
- Never send requests whose evident aim is something other than exercising the right (for example to set up compensation claims); that is the fact pattern in which a first request can lawfully be refused. The CJEU allows a pattern of many access requests followed by compensation claims against various controllers to count as evidence of abusive intention, so Unlist should not pair its requests with compensation demands.

**Complaints**

- Unlist drafts; the Subject lodges. Do not submit Complaints under the Operator's name.
- EU: target the Subject's own Supervisory Authority (residence), and note the Organization's country so a lead authority can be identified; for Organizations with no EEA establishment there is no lead authority. Only offer a Complaint about a request after that request was actually sent: from 2 April 2027 Reg. 2025/2518 Art. 4(3) makes this a condition for cross-border complaints, and the same order is sensible everywhere.
- UK: by default put a stage before the Complaint, because the regulator recommends it and its form asks for a copy — a complaint to the Organization under DPA 2018 s.164A, then (after the Organization's response, or after a reasonable time without one) the regulator, suggested within three months of the Organization's response. This is a recommendation, not a legal precondition: let the Subject skip it. The 30 days in s.164A is the Organization's deadline to *acknowledge*, not a waiting period set by law; using it as the default wait before escalating is a design choice.
- A Complaint draft needs: Subject's name and contact details, the Organization's identity and contacts, a chronological account, the infringement alleged (which request, which deadline missed), the remedy sought, whether the Organization was contacted first (with the exchanges, or why not), the attached correspondence including a copy of the sent request showing date and sender/recipient addresses, and, if someone other than the Subject files it, proof of authority.
- Avoid flooding one Supervisory Authority with many Complaints at once; batch or prioritise.
